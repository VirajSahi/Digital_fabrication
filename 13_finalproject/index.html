<div class="textcontainer"> <p class="margin"></p> <h3>Final Project:</h3> <h4 style="text-align:center"><u>The problem</u></h4><br> <div style="display: flex; align-items: flex-start; gap: 20px;"> <div style="flex: 1;"> <h5> In my hometown -<b> Punjab, India </b>- I constantly observe a complicated, culturally-rooted environmental issue: <b>religious river pollution.</b> Every year, 8 million tonnes of waste enter Indian rivers, a byproduct of household Hindu rituals. The waste includes pesticide-ridden fruits and flowers, plaster idols with lead paint, incense sticks, plastic, and more. People immerse these in rivers by the thousands to show their faith. The result – leaching toxins that cause Cancer, Hepatitis, and Dysentery in local communities, together constituting the leading cause of child mortality in India, as well as eutrophication and environmental harm. <br><br> When I was thirteen, I began visiting the riverbank every week, collecting two sacksful of trash. Over the years, people began to take notice, and our ranks rose to over 30 volunteers: this became Ghaggar Toli, a youth-led initiative to clean the Ghaggar River. We’ve collected over 16 tonnes of waste and conducted over 50 cleanups, working with schools, residential communities, NGOs, and the municipal government. However, <b>I can’t keep up with the trash</b>. It’s frustrating and demoralising to clean the river, only to find it relapse to its polluted state the next day. </h5> </div> <div style="flex: 0 0 300px;"> <img src="river_poll1.jpg" alt="Ghaggar River Cleanup" style="width: 480px; height: auto; border-radius: 8px;"> </div> </div> <h4 style="text-align:center"><u>The Solution</u></h4><br> <h5> My dream for Ghaggar Toli is to automate waste collection. That’s why I invented <b>Riverse:</b> a river-cleaning device to collect floating garbage before it leaches toxins. Riverse collects garbage through a 4-step process. First, it funnels floating waste towards the mouth of the collector using a trash boom barrier. Second, it rakes the trash to a coveyor belt, raising it out of contact with water. Third, it collects the waste in a bin and measures its volume and weight, sending SMS alerts for whe the bin is full. Fourth, it uses water wheels to power electronics and moving components, rendering the system cheaper, more robust, and energetically efficient. I used wooden rods for the rollers and 3D-printed parts for the chassis and stands, which I designed on Fusion 360. I wanted to avoid relying on friction alone to move the belt, as it could be faulty in water, so I wrapped strips of a teethed belt around the pins, tying the ends with fishing wire before supergluing them in place. I then glued 2 longer teethed belts to a piece of plastic acting as a coveyor belt, finally wrapped it onto the chassis. I used 2 metal gears to connect the head pulley to the water wheel, configuring the belt so that clockwise motion on the wheel would result in anticlockwise motion on the pulley. Lastly, I screwed all the components onto a wooden board using M4s. Since I was working with pvc pipes as roller pins, I 3D-printed cap which could fit onto the pipe’s end, secured with a M5x50 screw, with an embedded M8 lock nut at the tip. The lock nut screwed into a rotary bearing, whose housing I also desiged and 3d-printed. When connected at both sides, this created a smoothly rotating pulley. I created 5 of these to span the coveyor bed. I chose not to opt for a slider bed, as the friction could have interfered with the belt’s rotation. <br><br>I didn’t want to waste wood or metal to create a chassis, so I opted to create equally spaced stands for each PVC-pin, at heights ranging from 50 to 210mm, (Increments of 40mm), across a lenght of 400mm. I had originally wanted to 3d prit these stands; however, I realised I could lasercut them instead while retaining stability. I attached the bearings to these stands and created a makeshift conveyor. Initially, the stands shifted around when the pulleys rotated, so I had to lasercut a base on which they could be slotted in and glued. Lastly, I created a belt by stitchig together various plastics. I planned to attach half-cut coke cans to the rim to make it spin (see more in MCP week). To costruct this in real life, I planned to drill the coke cans on directly; however, the rim was impossible to drill or tap. Hence, I designed a 3D-printed bit that could fit onto the wheel snugly, tighten via screw, and attach to a can. After numerous iterations, it worked! I printed these en masse, attached 16 of them to each wheel, and finally cut, cleaned, and screwed on the aluminium cans. <br><br> Next, I designed 2 gears: one that fit onto the driver pulley of the conveyor, and another that attached to the water wheel. I made the gear ratio 1:5, so that the gently spiing water wheel could result in a high RPM for the pulley. I then 3D-printed a chain that attached both gears. I learnt too late that 3D-printed belts are incredibly finicky, so this did not end up working.
